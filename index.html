About me

After more than a decade as a multimedia developer and instructional designer in universities and hospitals, I had a sudden realization during the pandemic: the parts of my work I valued most were the moments when I could share what I knew with other people.

So, I went back to school to become a Technological Education teacher.

Since then, I have taught Technological Design, Architecture, Robotics, Communications Technology, STEM, Technology and the Skilled Trades, Civics and Careers, and I spent a year teaching MYP Design to Grades 6 to 10 at an international IB school in Madrid. Outside the classroom, I have served as design lead for a FIRST Robotics team, coached a student to fourth place provincially at Skills Ontario, and moderated clubs that included yearbook, photography, chess, band and various sports.

I see Technological Education as a discipline in which students learn to think by doing. My role in this context is to act as a guide on the side: to design meaningful problems, build the relationships that allow students to take risks, and help them reflect on what their process reveals about their learning. This is why I begin each course by asking students what they want to learn and by writing a classroom charter with them, and I share my own teaching philosophy with them so that they understand the reasons behind my practice. From an adult learning perspective, the same principles apply when working with teacher candidates.

Teaching philosophy

My teaching philosophy is, and likely always will be, a work in progress. This is because it draws on my academic, professional and personal experience to date and constantly changes as a result of this experience.

Broadly speaking, however, I see Technological Education as a discipline in which students learn to think by doing. I believe that education can advance social justice, equity and democracy, and that students learn most deeply when they reflect critically on hands-on experience.

In my view, treating learning as the acquisition of knowledge in the mind does little to explain how we learn hands-on skills, which is why I try to make theory concrete through project-based learning. In this context, I view my role as a teacher as a guide on the side, rather than a sage on the stage: to design meaningful problems, build the relationships that allow students to take risks, and help them reflect on what their process reveals about their own learning.

In my experience, positive relationships tend to matter more than the mastery of any single curriculum expectation or technology, because they are the foundation on which learning is built. I therefore treat the classroom as a learning community in which knowledge is co-constructed. I build that community from the first day, set expectations together with students, and rely on consistent routines rather than on rewards, threats, or demerits.

From an adult learning perspective, I believe the same principles apply to teacher candidates, who learn most when they connect new theory to the realities of their own classrooms and placements. The principles below guide my work with secondary students and adult learners alike, and each is paired with an example from my own teaching.

Principles in practice

Select a principle to read how it shapes my teaching, with an example from my own classroom.

01Student voice matters

My courses always begin with a learning expectations activity and a democratic vote on a classroom charter. I also invite students to ask me questions about myself. In response, I share a short presentation on who I am, which includes my teaching and learning philosophy, so that students understand why I teach the way I do. I find students tend to appreciate the transparency, and tend to be far more likely to own expectations that they helped to write.

02Every student can succeed

In my experience, confidence is a prerequisite for motivation. Many students arrive in Technological Education having been told, directly or indirectly, that they are not good at school. This is why in teacher’s college, I designed a classroom poster, “Mr. Coop’s Promise,” that tells students that anyone can earn an A+ in my class, and I try to make that promise credible by recognizing multiple intelligences, differentiating instruction and offering as much choice as possible in topics, formats, and classroom rules.

03Classrooms need to be safe places to fail

I treat iteration, testing, and redesign as evidence of learning, and I view “failure” as an opportunity rather than a personal shortcoming. In the Mechanical Design Challenge, for example, my brief told students that their designs could change during CAD and testing, and after competition day we reflected as a class on what each group had learned. I have also designed a Grade 11 lesson in which students analyse famous product failures, build paper towers against the clock, and then reflect on the mistakes they made and how they overcame them.

04Reflection is indispensable

Students might groan at writing reflections, but design logs, process journals, and critique help them articulate the decisions behind their work. My Technological Design and Architectural Design students kept weekly reflection journals, and my MYP students recorded every change to their plan in a production log. These journals not only give me ongoing feedback on learner progress, but also serve as a diagnostic tool for my own teaching, which I treat as a design process in its own right: I seek feedback from colleagues, rely on a network of collegial support, and make every effort to continue my professional learning, most recently through additional qualifications in Technological Design, an MYP Design teacher training course and my work as an associate teacher.

05Learning should be universally designed

I like to give students the opportunity to choose between tools such as Revit and SketchUp, work from structured templates and exemplars, and track their own progress with checklists. Just as importantly, I try to give students more than one way to show me what they know. I offer options in how students present their work rather than requiring a single format, and in my MYP Design classes, students could describe their finished monument in writing or in an audio recording. This matters because, as a teacher, seeing a student’s learning process is far more important to me than seeing their technological product. A polished model or video can hide a great deal, while a student explaining why they changed their design, in whatever format suits them best, shows me exactly what they have learned and where they still need support.

06Assessment ought to be authentic

Where possible, I always try to ensure that assessment tasks resemble the work of designers, producers, and builders in industry, and I like to share those criteria from the outset. In my classes, for example, students have worked as architects proposing a redesign of their school courtyard and as game designers responding to a client. I prioritize assessment for and as learning, offer multiple opportunities to submit formative work to reduce anxiety, and treat assessment as a dialogue with students about how to improve.

07Design can be leveraged for people, community and planet

I try my best to create projects that connect technical skill to social justice, community needs, and sustainability. Through this, I try to highlight the idea that technology is at once the source of, and a potential solution to, some of the world’s most pressing challenges. My students have proposed community housing projects for Windsor neighbourhoods using census data, designed textile products that promote the UN Sustainable Development Goals, and built games that raise awareness of social justice issues facing children.

08Indigenous knowledge and perspectives are invaluable

In my opinion, a broad-based view of Technological Education must make room for Indigenous knowledge in every course. After all, storytelling is itself a communication technology. In teacher’s college, I designed a Communications Technology lesson that connected Indigenous oral storytelling to contemporary journalism. When I researched the history of residential architecture in Canada, I also noticed that most sources began with colonial influences, as though nothing had been built here before, so I now look beyond that default starting point and try to learn directly from Indigenous journalists, producers, and communities.

09Engagement with technology and AI must be critical

Sound pedagogy should always drive the use of technology, rather than the reverse. Tools, including AI, need to be evaluated according to their purpose, content, and context. In my Short Film Project, for example, students were allowed to use generative AI for a first draft of their script but were expected to revise and reflect on it critically, and my robotics students debated the ethics of robotics and the future of work.

Equity in Technological Education

Technological Education has the potential to be one of the most equitable places in a school, but only when it is designed to be. Much of my recent teaching took place at a Windsor secondary school where many students were newcomers to Canada and were still learning English. In a design shop or a media lab, these students could often show what they knew through a sketch, a prototype, or a sequence of shots long before they could explain it in an essay. Through this experience, I came to see my responsibility as keeping language from becoming a barrier to the demonstration of knowledge, without lowering the expectations of the work itself.

In practice, as a teacher this means I try to demonstrate before explaining, pair technical vocabulary with images and translations, allow students to research and plan in their first language, and accept models, drawings and media as evidence of learning alongside writing. It also means recognizing that students arrive with very different levels of access to technology and very different prior experience with tools. In response, I have planned scaffolds, shared equipment, and supervised lab time so that every student can participate fully and safely.

Equity in Technological Education also concerns who sees a future in the field. I want students who are newcomers, multilingual, or new to technology to see design, media and the skilled trades as pathways that are open to them. From an adult learning perspective, I want the teacher candidates I work with to design their own programs with the same intention.
